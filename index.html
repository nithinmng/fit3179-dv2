Outdoor fitness in Australia · 2025 data

Outside is Australia's biggest gym

In 2025, six in ten Australian adults exercised in a park, street, beach or other public space. That is twice as many as used a gym or leisure centre. Who heads outdoors, what keeps people inside, and how much room is there to move?

01

The open-air gym

Ask Australians where they exercised last year and one answer dominates. Parks, footpaths, beaches and trails beat any single gym, pool or club category.

02

Who's out there

The open-air gym is not one crowd. What people do changes with age, women and men use public space differently, and household income shapes how often anyone gets moving at all.

03

Why, when and how

Most adults are active at least partly on their own terms, not only through clubs. Health is the top reason to move, and being outdoors is among the top four. For people who stay inactive, the weather is rarely the problem. The seasons still set the rhythm, and each capital runs on a different calendar.

04

Room to move

If public space is Australia's biggest gym, where is the equipment and where is the space? Two datasets answer two different questions here, and they are not equally complete.

Read this first: two kinds of map

Outdoor gyms (charts 14 and 15) come from OpenStreetMap, kept up by volunteers, so they show mapped outdoor gyms, not every one that exists. Only about a third of the sites in the City of Gold Coast's own 2015 Fitness Stations list appear on it, and mapping effort likely differs between places. Use these maps to see where mapped gyms cluster, not to compare councils.

Park space (charts 16 to 18) comes from official ABS land-use and population data, which covers every council. It measures how much local parkland exists per resident, not how good, safe or reachable it is.

What it adds up to

Australia's favourite gym has no doors. Keeping it open means keeping room to move.

Most adults use public space to stay active, and that habit grows with age until the mid-60s. But who gets moving still tracks money: weekly activity rises steeply with household income. Space is a different story. Across the capital cities, local park space per resident is only weakly related to how disadvantaged a council is, and much more closely tied to density. Several of the tightest councils are among the least disadvantaged inner suburbs, while outer suburbs have more room per person.

Keeping the open-air gym open means protecting local parkland where people are packing in, and making it easy for everyone, at every income, to use the space they already have.

Data & method

What "local parkland" means here

The ABS sorts every mesh block, Australia's smallest statistical area, by its main land use. This page counts mesh blocks classed as Parkland that are 50 hectares (0.5 km²) or smaller as local parkland, then divides that area by each council's population. Larger parkland blocks are mostly regional parks, bushland reserves and national parks, which would make fringe councils look park-rich even when little of that land is a short walk from home. The cut-off applies to the size of the mesh block, not the size of the park, and small parks inside mesh blocks classed as residential are not counted.

The cut-off is a judgement, so the finding was checked at 20 hectares, 50 hectares and 2 km². Across the 135 capital-city councils, park space per resident barely relates to disadvantage at any cut-off (rank correlation between −0.05 and −0.20), but it falls clearly as councils get denser (between −0.56 and −0.86; −0.75 at the 50 hectare cut-off used on this page). A rank correlation runs from −1 to 1; values near 0 mean no consistent link.

Land use is from the 2021 ABS mesh blocks; population is the ABS estimate for 30 June 2025. Parks rarely change quickly, but new estates since 2021 are not reflected. Parkland covers parks, reserves and sports grounds of every kind; it does not tell you whether a space is accessible, safe, shaded or well kept, and nearby land in a neighbouring council is not counted.

Other things to know

  • AusPlay is the national sport and physical activity survey run by the Australian Sports Commission. Answers are self-reported for the past 12 months, and small groups (NT, ACT, 15 to 17 year olds) carry wider margins of error. AusPlay moved from phone to online in 2023, so these figures cannot be compared with releases before then. The next release (July 2025 to June 2026) is scheduled for late October 2026.
  • "Public space" is one of AusPlay's own categories for where an activity was done. This page describes it with everyday examples such as parks, streets and beaches; those examples are ours, not AusPlay's. It is not a measure of outdoor gym use.
  • OpenStreetMap outdoor gyms are a volunteer-mapped snapshot (24 October 2026): 2,174 features merged into 1,745 sites. They are shown only as mapped locations.
  • SEIFA scores are from 2021; 545 of 548 councils matched the 2025 boundaries (Merri-bek uses its former Moreland score; two NT councils have none: Groote Archipelago, created in 2023, and East Arnhem, whose boundaries changed at the same time).
  • Climate figures are long-term monthly averages for one weather station per capital, over different periods.
  • Maps use an equal-area conic projection centred on Australia (standard parallels 18°S and 36°S), so areas are compared fairly.
